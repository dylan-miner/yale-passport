# Minimum Viable Product

**Product Management Foundations (ENAS 3110a)**
Yale University · Fall Semester 2026

## Overview

- The course will have one major project - the minimum viable product (MVP)
- The MVP is a group project. Students form teams of ~4 and work on the project together.
- The MVP will be comprised of multiple smaller projects which students complete over the span of the course
- These milestones are designed to make sure students are on track for the final deliverable. In order, they are:
  - Problem
  - Solution/Design
  - Launch
  - Roadmap
- These 4 parts will be combined at the end of the class into the final deliverable which has 3 components:
  - MVP Document
  - Product review
  - Working MVP app: a functioning version of the product you designed that a real user can use for its core flow
- The teaching team will provide feedback on each part individually as we move through the course so the students have an opportunity to improve them for the final submission. The project is worth 90% of your final grade: each of the 4 milestones is worth 10%, and the final MVP (document and working app) and Product Review together are worth 50%.

## Problem Space (Draft due Sep 25, Final due Oct 9)

### Overview

- One of the most common failures in product management (and problem solving in general) is conflating a solution with a problem. Many projects have died due to someone saying "what if we build X" before they asked "what problem are we solving and why is it important."
- It's absolutely critical to separate the problem space from the solution space and to think about what problem you're solving before you think about how to solve it.
- A key part of understanding the problem is knowing who you are solving the problem for.
  - You may pick any customer you want. There are no restrictions on who your customer can be
  - We're keeping this class focused on the "real economy."
    - There is no restriction based on who your customer is, where they work, or whether their job exists outside a university
    - Examples of customer groups that have worked well in past classes:
      - Facilities manager, transportation manager, healthcare administrator, librarian, support technician, physician, IT manager, gardener, etc.
    - The one real constraint is access: you must be able to find and interview real people in this group:
      - Groups you already have a way into, through a job, an internship, family, a team, or a club, are usually the easiest to reach.
    - We do recommend focusing on your customers in their business or professional context. From doing this multiple times, we've found it much simpler for students to focus on a business product than a consumer product.
    - Use your judgment on what's reasonable and if you aren't sure, ask.
  - A note on team access:
    - Pick a group that is identifiable and reasonably common, so you are not relying on a single hard-to-reach person.
      - The key factor is access. Your team has limited time - if several people on your team can speak with your customer, it will help tremendously.
    - Tell the teaching team as early as possible what you're thinking so we can make sure you're on the right track.
- In addition to solving a real problem, products in the real world must also be a business opportunity. If your product can't capture some of the value from the problem it's solving, it won't be self-sustaining. You must understand the market opportunity and how you will make money (in rough terms).
  - This is not a "startup" class and we are not pretending to be venture capitalists. Success for this project does not require a billion dollar market or a hundred million customers.
  - We care that you accurately understand reality, not that the reality matches the requirements of a venture backed startup.
- A critical part of assessing the opportunity is understanding who/what else is attempting to solve this problem.
- This is a great opportunity to think about what success looks like. What's your criteria for being satisfied with the project? If you don't set this now in writing, it will morph over time.

### Key Skills

- Problems come before solutions. Don't conflate the two.
- Understand your customer, both who they are and what they care about.
- Learn to think like someone other than you. Don't describe how you think your customer should feel/act, describe how they feel and act.
- Why is the problem also an opportunity? How will you be able to capture value?
- What does your competition do well and where are the gaps? Don't fall prey to the lazy trap of saying "our competition sucks." Be specific and intellectually honest with your analysis.

### Deliverable

- Due dates: draft due Sep 25 before class starts; final due Oct 2 before class starts. The draft is not graded. Your team's mentor will read it and give you extensive feedback to incorporate before you submit the final, which is graded.
- Deliverable is a written document (5 pages max)
  - More is not better. It's critical to write, distill, and rewrite your work until you have the minimum required to convey your point.
  - Consider structuring this deliverable (and future deliverables) like this document - bullet points and short sentences.
- Answer the following questions (feel free to use as a template):
  - Who is your target customer(s)?
    - How can you uniquely identify them from the population at large?
    - What do they care about?
  - What problem are you solving for them and why is it important?
    - Include customer quotes/research
    - Think from the perspective of the customer not your perspective
    - How will you measure success for solving this problem, quantitatively and qualitatively?
  - Why is it an opportunity for you? Will you be able to capture some of the value?
    - Include estimates of TAM, SAM, TM
      - These must be in dollars (not people, etc.)
    - We don't require a full business model but you should make a persuasive case that there is economic value to be captured
  - What else attempts to solve this problem (competition)?
    - Where is the competition strong and where is it weak?
    - Don't just say competition is "bad" - talk through the eyes of your customers about why existing solutions aren't sufficient

## Solution/Design (Due Oct 30)

### Overview

- Now that you understand the problem you're solving and why it's important, it's time to figure out how you want to solve it. This is where we introduce solutions.
- It's vital to narrow down the possible design space before your exploration so you have a more bounded problem. Use design constraints that correlate with the problem space to narrow the set of possible designs.
  - Design constraints will also help you avoid "taste" as an argument. "Because we like it" is not a good reason - how will it help your customers and why will they like it?
  - Example: Your customers are color blind therefore designs can't include red and green next to each other
- Once you have a bounded design space, good PMs will explore many possible solutions to understand if there's a better solution they haven't considered. Diverge and come up with many ideas before you converge and pick one.
- The primary purpose of the design sprint is to have frequent check-ins with reality to confirm your understanding of your customers. It's crucial to document what you're learning so that your customer model constantly improves.
- It's ok to not have everything figured out but the shape should be there
  - Communicate the general structure, flow, and key inputs

### Key Skills

- Using design constraints to restrict your explorations. Show us how the needs of your customers put some logical constraints on possible solutions.
- Diverging and coming up with many solutions that explore a meaningful portion of all reasonable options
- Converging and picking one solution
- Communicating designs visually
  - We are tool agnostic (you can use paper/pencil, Sketch, Balsamiq, Photoshop, Powerpoint, Paint, whatever) but you must clearly convey how your product will look and work.
- Communicating a self contained design at the right level of detail (not too much, not too little). This deliverable should be able to stand on its own with the general shape of your solution.

### Deliverable

- Due date: Oct 16 before class starts
- Deliverable is 1 written document and 1 visual document
- Unlike the other assignments, there are no length restrictions due to the visual nature of the deliverable
  - Brevity still applies. You should not be submitting 25 page documents.
- Visual document must be a PDF! We need to guarantee we can see the images.
- Design Constraints
  - Concisely explain your design constraints
  - Explain why they are tied back to your fundamental understanding of the problem and not arbitrary restrictions
- Divergence
  - Show the major different explorations you did to cover the possible design space
    - These do not need to be in high fidelity or completely specified
      - The goal is to only convey the essence of the design and why it's different from the other options
- Convergence
  - Explain how users reacted to the different designs. What resonated, what didn't, and what did you learn?
  - Explain why/how you chose your final design
- Final Design
  - Show your final design visually
    - Looking for wireframe fidelity (or better) sketches
      - No bonus points for higher fidelity than wireframe
  - Minimize cognitive overhead for your recipients
    - Use the Pareto Principle and don't include every detail.
    - Focus on the things that matter the most and your core interactions.
    - It's ok to let the reader fill in the less important details in their own head.
  - A reasonable person should be able to figure out how your product will work from this document alone
    - Feel free to include text on this document but keep it short and simple

## Launch Plan (Due Nov 9)

### Overview

- There are many examples from history where the best product doesn't win. Products require distribution to end up in the hands of customers.
- A good launch plan should understand which methods are likely to cost effectively get your product to your customers.
- Products also need to be explained to the customer. Very rarely will they "just get it." A good launch plan needs to clearly articulate a problem that resonates with the customer and explain how the product will solve that problem for them.
  - This has lots of overlap with Product Marketing but good PMs need a solid understanding of this discipline.
- Launches must also be measured. It's easy to claim success but much harder to actually hit quantifiable goals. A launch plan should have clear explanations for channels and cost to prevent the team from being intellectually dishonest.

### Key Skills

- Clearly explaining the product to the target customer in a way that resonates
- Understanding and planning for effective distribution
- Quantifying success and establishing ahead of time what will be measured and why

### Deliverable

- Due date: Nov 9 11:30AM
- 4 pages max
- Should clearly answer the following questions:
  - Blog post
    - How do you explain your product to your target customer so that they understand and desire it?
      - Present an actual blog post that you would show to a customer (1 page max)
        - This is not in bullet points. It's something you'd hand to a customer and should communicate your project and the benefits to someone outside your organization.
        - The target audience is your customer. It should resonate with them.
        - Try to avoid something that "sounds like PR."
      - Should clearly cover the following:
        - Who is the product for?
        - What is the primary benefit?
        - How is it different from competition?
      - Feel free to use images and diagrams
  - Economics
    - Estimate the lifetime value (LTV) of a customer
      - It's ok to be rough but show your work and assumptions
      - This should be the value (in dollars) of a single customer
      - Make sure you estimate LTV using margin not revenue
        - If you have a software product, it's ok to lookup estimates of software company margins and apply those to your revenue
        - If you have a hardware product or something with very significant costs, use Fermi estimation to approximate your margins
      - Once you have the lifetime value, clearly tell us how much you're willing to spend to acquire a customer to have a healthy ratio
        - This should be the maximum dollar value you would pay for one customer
  - Distribution
    - How will you distribute your product?
      - What channels (3) will you use and why are they appropriate for your product?
        - These can include in-product channels like referrals, etc.
        - Explain why these channels are uniquely suited to your product. Don't just pick vanilla things. Show us that you put thought into what's uniquely suitable for your product.
      - For each channel, estimate how much it would cost to acquire a single user and compare it to the allowable number you previously calculated
        - We'd like to see math and a final dollar value. Rough numbers are ok
        - The number you calculate should be the dollar value to acquire 1 customer through each channel
        - Feel free to use internet estimates for things like ad costs, click-through rates, etc. Cite your sources
        - It's ok to conclude that a channel is not cost effective

## Roadmap (Due Nov 20)

### Overview

- Roadmaps are a key part of product management. Good PMs must have a plan for the future of their product.
- Large Excel sheets of individual projects do not make good roadmaps. The cognitive overhead of understanding dozens of projects is too high and large lists very rarely succeed.
- Good roadmaps will group projects into themes and clearly explain why the top themes are critical to the customer/business and then clearly prioritize projects within each theme.
  - People require stories they can tell themselves and their colleagues to put their work in context and understand why what they are doing is important
  - Make sure your roadmap isn't selfish. A roadmap cannot only deliver value to the company, it must also deliver value to the customer. Your customers don't care if your margins are bad or customer support costs are too high. You must have a blend of both to be successful.
  - Themes should be problems not solutions.
- Prioritization is absolutely critical. There can only be a small number of priorities and a small number of projects. We're looking for students to demonstrate that they have been rigorous about prioritization and not include more things than necessary.
- Good roadmaps will also attempt to understand the cost/benefit of each project/theme but at the appropriate level of granularity.
  - The most common flaw is garbage in garbage out.
  - PMs provide extremely detailed estimates of cost and impact which are usually both wrong. This variance compounds across many projects and the priority order often ends up making no sense.
  - We are once again looking for Fermi estimation to capture the probability spread of a project vs. a very particular point. Consider estimating impact/cost as small, medium, large, or on a scale of 1-5 vs many significant digits.
  - This varies based on the project and the information the PM has
- Good roadmaps can also stand on their own which means projects need good names which convey the essence of the project
  - Codenames/very long project names make it hard for a reader to understand the project
  - Consider how to label items on your roadmap so that they are pithy and concise and clearly communicate the project from the title/short description
- Good roadmaps also recognize that judgment matters and that you cannot create a roadmap purely by estimating numbers.

### Key Skills

- Prioritization
  - PMs must make hard tradeoffs and be able to justify them
  - Poor prioritization kills projects. There can only be a few priorities at any point in time
- Aggregation
  - It's important to combine projects into themes which are easy to understand and are demonstrated to be important
  - We are looking for logical aggregation which promotes telling a clear story (vs. many small projects) and clearly explains why each theme is important.
- Clear and simple impact/cost at the right level of granularity for the project
- Concise project explanation.
  - A reader must be able to tell at a glance what a project is on the roadmap without reading a long supporting doc

### Deliverable

- Due date: Nov 20 before class starts
- Your deliverable should be 3 pages max
- The roadmap should describe a delta between your "Current Product" and your "Future Product."
  - Your "Current Product" is what you turned in as your design milestone
  - The roadmap should describe differences between that state and where you'd like to be
  - In general, you should not explain how you are going to prioritize what you turned in for the design sprint.
    - The teaching team will reach out if you had a particularly complex design sprint and we want you to prioritize your listed features
- Mark which projects you will build into your working MVP app by Dec 21 (your MVP scope) and which come after it. Keep the MVP scope to the smallest set that makes the core flow work.
- Every part of the roadmap should demonstrate intentionality
  - Anytime you are making a major decision, make sure to explain why
- It should clearly lay out a small number of themes (3-5)
  - Themes should be problems not solutions
    - Think of them as sub-problems of your problem deliverable
  - Themes should have a clear articulation of why they are important.
    - Tell us why you picked these themes out of all the ones you could have picked
  - Themes should be prioritized relative to each other with a clear rationale
- Each theme should contain a small number of projects (3-5)
  - The projects should be in priority order and should have estimates of impact and cost where possible
  - Where not possible, clearly explain why you've chosen to work on this project and convince us you were intentional in your reasoning
    - Example: A future project has a dependency on this one
- Projects should be the right balance of general and specific
  - They should be bigger than tasks but smaller than themes
  - Think of them as goals you could accomplish in the solution space that if achieved, would fulfill your theme
  - Projects should be clearly titled/described and an informed reader should be able to roughly understand the shape of the project at a glance from the roadmap.

## Product Review and MVP (Due Dec 11; working MVP app due Dec 21)

### Overview

- Over the course of the class, you've received feedback on each section of your MVP (problem space, design, roadmap, and launch).
- It's time to bring together everything you've worked on this semester into the 3 different parts of the final project: the product review, the MVP document, and the working MVP app.
- PMs must be effective advocates for their ideas both in person and in writing.
- PMs must also ship. An MVP is the smallest version of your product that actually works for a real user, not only a description of it.
- The final project consists of the following:
  - Product review
    - 18 minute presentation on your project, including a live demo of your MVP app as it stands on Dec 11
  - MVP document
    - Combines the 4 prior projects into a single, written document outlining your product
    - Remember to include the blog post that you have from the Launch Plan assignment in the MVP
    - Use this opportunity to revise and iterate based on teaching team feedback and things you've learned along the way.
  - Working MVP app
    - A working version of your final design that a target customer can use to complete the core flow end to end
- Product review
  - Everyone has their own style - there is no generic "effective" style of presenting
  - The key to success is telling a clear and compelling story about why this is a real problem, why it's a real opportunity, and how you are going to solve the problem.
- MVP document
  - The MVP document should be even more concise than your previous documents and succinctly lay out a case for why you are solving an important problem and how you are going to solve it.
  - Iteration is key to being an effective PM. This is your chance to iterate on what you've learned.
- Working MVP app
  - Your design milestone showed how the product should work. The app shows that it does. Build the core flow, not every feature.

### Key Skills

- Effective presenting
- Iterating
  - Revise and consolidate based on feedback from teaching team + peers
- Building
  - Turn your design into a working product scoped to the core flow, and cut everything that isn't needed to solve the core problem

### Deliverable

- Due date
  - Product review: Dec 11
  - MVP document: Dec 11
  - Working MVP app: Dec 21
- Percent of final grade: 50%

### Product Review

- Guidelines
  - Focus on clear storytelling and remember that your audience doesn't have all the information you have
    - Framing will be crucial - the way you tee up the problem and your designs will make or break your presentation.
    - Set clear and appropriate context at the right level of granularity for your audience
  - Practice until it's seamless
    - Being a good PM involved being a good storyteller and a lot of work behind the scenes to make your product reviews feel seamless
  - Tell the story, don't recap your work.
    - This is not a post-mortem - your goal is to clearly explain what problem you're solving, why it's important to the customer, why it's an opportunity, how you are going to solve it, and how you are going to launch and grow that product.
    - Don't tell us what you did to get here, just tell us the final answer
- Format
  - Presentation
    - Should include slides + talk track + a live demo of your MVP app (a work in progress is fine; the final app is due Dec 21)
    - Please use Google Slides for your presentation
      - This will let us provide comments and queue up presentations on a single laptop
    - Have a short screen recording of the demo ready as a backup in case something breaks live
  - 18 minute time limit
    - Strictly enforced so we have time for everyone
- Expected Sections
  - Problem
    - Who is your customer?
      - What uniquely identifies them vs. someone who isn't your customer?
    - What problem are you solving for them?
      - Paint a picture with real interactions and proof points from your interviews
      - Make sure to provide appropriate context for your classmates who will not be experts on your customers.
    - Why is the problem an opportunity?
      - Explain your market opportunity (TAM, SAM, TM)
    - What does success look like?
      - Layout your success metrics
  - Solution
    - What does your minimum viable product look like?
      - Demo your MVP app as it stands on Dec 11.
        - No need to tell us how you got here just explain the final product
      - Walk through only the core flow of your app that solves the core problem you identified
        - No need to cover edge cases or basic steps (signing up, resetting password, etc.)
  - Roadmap
    - What is your roadmap?
      - Explain your themes
        - What are they?
        - Why did you prioritize them?
        - Use quantitative and qualitative info where possible?
      - Don't explain the individual projects
        - Too detailed for this sort of review
  - Launch
    - What is your expected LTV?
    - Which channels will you use to distribute your product?
      - For each channel, why is it the right one?
        - Why is it uniquely suited to your target customer?
        - Will the economics be reasonable?
        - Compare expected CAC to LTV

### MVP Document

- Guidelines
  - Combine your 4 previous documents (Problem, Design, Roadmap, Launch) into one master document
    - Should be a GDoc like the previous assignments
    - Blog post in the Launch Plan assignment should be included
    - 12 pages max plus designs
      - The skill here is knowing what to keep and what to cut
  - Provide a link to your working MVP app at the top of the document (see Working MVP App below)
  - Grading criteria for the document are identical to previous projects

### Working MVP App

- Guidelines
  - Build a working version of your final design that a target customer can use to complete the core flow end to end
    - A clickable mockup or Figma prototype is not enough. The core flow must actually work: real inputs, real outputs, data that is saved.
  - Goal: earn $100 from your product by Dec 21. One customer can pay $100, or several customers can pay amounts that add up to $100.
    - Payments must come from real target customers, not teammates, friends or family
    - When you submit the app on Dec 21, tell us how much you earned, from whom, and what that taught you about the opportunity
  - Any tools are fine, including no-code tools and AI coding tools
  - Scope it with your roadmap: build the projects you marked as your MVP scope
  - Make sure the teaching team can open it
    - Submit a link that works without special access, or include a test login
- What we look for
  - The core flow works end to end without hand-holding
  - It matches the design and MVP scope in your MVP document, or your Dec 21 submission explains what changed and why
  - It solves the core problem for the customer you defined
